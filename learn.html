What RAPP is

You write one file. Your AI can do a new thing. That’s the whole idea, and everything else on this page is in service of it.
RAPP — Rapid Agent Prototype Platform.

An agent

One file. A description of what it does, and one function that does it.

No project, no folder, no build. A docstring so a person understands it, a manifest so a machine does, and a perform() that returns a string. That’s an agent.

So teaching your AI to check the tide is one file, and you can send it to someone the way you’d send a photo.

A brainstem

The thing that holds your agents and talks to you.

It runs on your own machine, on the AI seat you already pay for. Drop an agent in and it can use it on the next thing you ask. Nothing to deploy, no new bill.

So your AI grows by accumulation, and what it can do is a folder you own.

A registry

Where agents are shared, under whoever wrote them.

Someone else already wrote the one you need. You take it, or you publish yours and somebody takes it from you. Same one-file shape either way, which is the only reason that works.

So the thing gets better while you sleep, because other people are using it.

The obvious worry

You are about to run a stranger’s file on your own machine. Ask about that.

It is the right question and it deserves a straight answer rather than a reassuring one. An agent is one readable file — you can look at it, which is the first defence and the reason the one-file rule exists at all. Beyond that: agents fetched from a registry are checked against a published fingerprint and refused if the bytes do not match, rather than repaired or run with a warning. But a file that matches its fingerprint is still a file that does what it says, and what it says might be something you did not want. Read it, or run it somewhere you do not mind losing.

The honest version: this is the same trust you extend to any package you install, with one advantage — it is short enough to actually read.

That’s it. That’s the part to teach

  1. One file is one new ability.
  2. Your own machine, your own seat.
  3. Agents are shared, so nobody starts from nothing.

And this is the body

The words below are how that scales past one machine — to many agents, many devices, real or virtual, all agreeing about what happened. They are not pipework. The format calls a stream of these “an organism’s biography”, and it means it: what follows is a skeleton, a pulse, a way of having offspring, and a way of being in the room from somewhere else.

The join

Every time an agent’s perform() runs, that is one moment — and the moment gets written down as a frame.

That is the whole bridge, and it is the sentence the rest of this page hangs from. Your tide-checking file runs; a frame says it ran, what it saw, and what it depended on. Six of those in a row is a life. Everything below is what becomes possible once moments are written down instead of forgotten.

Frame

A frame is a moment, written down so it can’t be quietly changed later.

It says what was true and what it depended on, and carries its own fingerprint, so anyone can check it wasn’t edited — including you, in a year, when you’ve forgotten. Everything else here stands on it, which is the sense in which it is a skeleton rather than a note.

Like a bone: you don’t look at it, but nothing stands up without it.

Tick

A tick is one frame after another. That’s all time is here.

It looks, it decides, it acts, and the moment gets written down. String the ticks together and you have a life you can read back — which is why the format calls it a biography and not a log.

Like a pulse. Not a metaphor for one — it is what makes the thing alive rather than paused.

Wear

A key you can say out loud wears a frame down into a smaller world — a tile.

A tile is what’s left when the wearing is done: a small, complete, particular place, carved out of a big general record. Give the same frame and the same key to two computers that have never met and they carve exactly the same tile. Nothing travelled between them except the words — which is what it’s for: sending somebody a whole situation in a text message that arrives identical on their machine, with neither of you uploading anything.

Like Michelangelo and the block of marble. The David was already in there; the work was taking away everything that wasn’t him. A frame is the block. The key is which figure you’re carving toward. What’s left standing is the tile — and unlike marble, the same block and the same key give the same statue to everyone, every time, forever.

Slosh

Pour one thing through another and it comes out changed.

Pour a world through “low gravity” and things in it start to drift. Pour a creature through that same world and the creature comes out with a longer reach. It is one operation, and which of the two is being changed is your choice — that is what it is for: building up a situation, or a character, one condition at a time instead of specifying it all at once.

Like eating. What passes through changes what it passes through, and which of the two is the food depends on the day.

Cross

Cross the same two things many different ways, then keep what came out least like either parent.

Mixing two things once gets you a blend — mostly one, a bit of the other. So cross them a dozen ways at once and let each attempt go somewhere of its own. Then, wherever the attempts disagree, select the answer furthest from where you started. Averaging them would undo the whole point.

Like breeding, because it is breeding. Two parents, many crosses, and you keep the one that is nobody’s duplicate — which is how you get a new thing instead of a slightly blurred old one.

Holo

You can be in the room without being in the room.

Each agent says where it is standing. Every world paints everyone else as a projection. They can be in completely different places and still be together — and it looks like a projection on purpose, because something that pretends to be solid is lying the moment the connection drops.

Like a video call where everyone stands in your room instead of in little boxes — and still looks like light, so you never mistake it for a body.

The actual test

  1. Close this page.
  2. Explain the first three to somebody. Agent, brainstem, registry. That’s RAPP.
  3. Ask them to explain it back.
  4. Whatever they got wrong is what this page got wrong — not what they got wrong.
  5. Then teach the join — a perform() runs, a frame says it ran. One sentence, and it is what makes the rest of this page one thing instead of two.
  6. The other six are how that works once there is more than one machine. Not optional, just later.

If you want to build with it rather than talk about it, the format is rapp/1 and the derivation is written down in WEARING.md, with test vectors so your implementation can prove itself instead of hoping. Everything here is free to use and gets better the more people use it — that’s the point of the ground being fertile rather than fenced.
